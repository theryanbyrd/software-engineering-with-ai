# The "We Are Not Pivoting" All-Hands

The all-hands or written communication when:
- A competitor just announced something flashy
- Your team is anxious — Slack is going off, individual ICs are messaging you, your senior engineers are asking
- Your CEO is anxious — they're forwarding the launch tweet, asking what you're going to do
- The board is asking — investors are asking the board, the board is asking the CEO

This template holds the line. It is not the "no, that announcement is dumb" message; it's the calmer, more durable "we're going to evaluate this on our timeline, not the news cycle" message.

## When to use

- A direct competitor (or near-peer) made a public announcement that touches your domain
- The announcement has produced visible anxiety on your team within 48 hours
- You have *not* validated the announcement against your own data yet
- Your strategy is still defensible based on what you knew last week

## When NOT to use

- The competitor's announcement is genuinely a category-killer for what you're building. (This is rare; announcements rarely live up to themselves. But when it happens, the template is wrong; you do need to pivot.)
- The announcement reveals you've been making a strategic error you should have caught. (Then have the harder conversation about the error.)
- The team's anxiety is legitimate and your strategy is no longer defensible. (Listen first; this template is for "your strategy is fine but the team is panicking," not for "your strategy needs revision.")

If you're not sure which scenario you're in, take 24-48 hours before responding publicly. The all-hands message can wait two days; a wrong message cannot be recalled.

## The all-hands message (verbatim, ~3-4 minutes spoken)

### Opening (90 seconds)

> "I want to address [competitor / vendor announcement] directly.
>
> First — I've seen the announcement. The team has seen it. We're not going to pretend we didn't.
>
> Second — we are not pivoting our strategy this week. I'll explain what that means, what we *are* doing, and why I think the calmer response is the right one.
>
> Third — I'd rather be 90% confident and right than 50% confident and fast. Most of the panic-pivots I've watched in this industry over the last 18 months ended badly. Most of the calm responses ended fine. We're going to be in the second category."

### What the announcement is and isn't (60 seconds)

> "[Competitor] announced [specific thing]. What we know: [what's verifiable from public information]. What we don't know: [what hasn't been validated — internal usage data, customer outcomes, durability of the claim].
>
> I want to specifically push back on a category of reaction that I've already heard in Slack: 'we should be doing this.' The right question is not whether [competitor] is doing it; the right question is whether OUR work is improved by doing it. Sometimes the answer is yes; sometimes the answer is no; sometimes the answer is 'yes but not the way they're doing it.' We don't know yet.
>
> What I'd specifically caution against: assuming that because [competitor] announced [thing], they have it working in production at scale, customers love it, and they're racing ahead. Announcements are theater; the implementation is the actual work."

### What we're doing (60 seconds)

> "Three things specifically over the next 2-3 weeks:
>
> One: I've asked [senior IC] to lead a small evaluation. They'll talk to [N] customers, look at [data we have access to], and give us a written read in [date]. The goal is to understand what's real, what's theater, and where the genuine signal is for our work.
>
> Two: I'm checking with the engineering leadership team about whether anything we'd already planned for [next quarter] needs reordering in light of this. Not a re-plan; a sanity check on the current plan.
>
> Three: I'm staying close to [our customers / our team / our specific competitive surface] for the next 30 days to listen for whether the announcement actually changes anyone's behavior, or whether it was a news cycle that fades."

### What I want from you (60 seconds)

> "Two things.
>
> One: please continue to do the work we agreed to do. Slack debate is fine; we'll have a real conversation in [day] when we have the data. But the team that pivots its plan based on a competitor's launch keynote rarely ends up where it wanted to be.
>
> Two: if you have specific information about [competitor's announcement] — you've used it, you know an engineer there, you have customer signal — please send it to me directly. The data points everyone has are public; the data points only you have are valuable. I want them.
>
> Three [if asked, but only if asked] — the leadership team is calm. We will tell you if our position changes. If you don't hear from us, it's not because we're hiding something; it's because we're letting the dust settle and looking at the data.
>
> Questions?"

## When the team pushes back

### "But everyone is talking about [thing]"

> "Including us. That's normal. The question isn't whether to discuss it; it's whether to act on it. Discussion costs us 15 minutes; action without data can cost us a quarter."

### "Don't we need to move fast?"

> "Move fast on what? If you mean 'investigate quickly and decide,' yes — that's the [N]-day window I described. If you mean 'announce something so we look responsive,' no. The companies that announce responsively to a competitor's news cycle look weak in 60 days."

### "But we're going to fall behind"

> "Behind on what? On the metrics that matter for our work, our position is [specific position]. The competitor's announcement doesn't move our position; it moves their narrative. Those are different. We respond to position changes; we don't respond to narrative changes.
>
> If the competitor's announcement turns into market position over 60-90 days — meaning customers actually choose them over us because of it — we'll respond. We have time."

### "What if our investors / board ask us about it?"

> "They will. The board comm I'll send is essentially the same as this all-hands message — we've seen it, we're evaluating, our strategy holds, we'll update if data warrants. Boards generally appreciate this framing more than panic-driven pivots; they've seen the pattern."

### "What if our customers ask us about it?"

> "Some will. The customer talking points I'll send are essentially: 'yes, we've seen it; here's how our approach differs; here's why we believe ours holds.' If you're in a customer conversation and they ask about [announcement], reach out to [sales / CS leadership] for the latest framing. Don't improvise."

### "What if [senior engineer] is right and we should pivot?"

> "[Senior engineer] is welcome to argue for that. The format I'd ask for: a written memo, max one page, with what would change in our roadmap, what it would cost, what would be at risk if we don't, and what evidence supports the claim. I'll read it; the leadership team will read it; we'll respond.
>
> What I won't accept: a pivot driven by Slack momentum without a written argument. The discipline is the same as for any other large bet — write it down, defend it, get critique. Big decisions made on Slack tend not to age well."

## The written companion (Slack post or internal blog)

If the all-hands isn't the right format for your company — too small for a meeting, too remote, wrong cadence — adapt to writing:

> **Re: [competitor announcement]**
>
> Quick note since folks are asking.
>
> 1. **What we know:** [public information about the announcement]
> 2. **What we don't yet know:** [the implementation, customer outcomes, durability]
> 3. **What we're doing:** [senior IC] is leading a 2-3 week evaluation. We'll have a written read by [date]. Until then, we are not pivoting.
> 4. **Why we're not pivoting today:** the cost of a wrong pivot is much higher than the cost of waiting 2 weeks for data. Most of the panic-pivots I've watched in this industry ended badly; most of the calm responses ended fine.
> 5. **What I'd love from you:** specific data points only you have (you've used the product, you know someone there, you have customer signal) — send them to me direct. Aggregate Slack debate is welcome but lower-value.
> 6. **When you'll hear from me again:** [date] with the eval result. Earlier if something material happens.
>
> [your name]

## What to do if the announcement *was* a category change

This template is wrong if the announcement is genuinely game-changing. Signals that it might be:

- **Customer signal within 48 hours.** Multiple customers proactively asking about the announcement (not because you sent it to them, but because they're considering moving).
- **Material technical capability you can't replicate in 6 months.** A 2x speedup is not category change. A capability that requires technology you don't have is.
- **A 5+ point improvement on your internal benchmark** (if applicable). This is the threshold from Ch 27.
- **A pricing change that fundamentally alters the unit economics of your business.** Rare but real.

If two of these are true, this template is wrong. Run a different conversation: a leadership-team off-site, a written re-plan, a board comm that names the situation honestly. The "we are not pivoting" message in those circumstances is denial, not discipline.

## What this template will NOT do

- Will not work if your strategy was already weak. The template buys you time to evaluate, not time to fix a strategy that wasn't right to begin with.
- Will not work if the team has lost confidence in your judgment from prior failures. The pre-condition is credibility.
- Will not work as a permanent answer. You committed to a real evaluation in 2-3 weeks; deliver on that. Templates that promise data and don't produce data are worse than no template.

## Setup before the conversation

1. **Read the announcement carefully.** Not the keynote summary — the actual specifics. Often the announcement is more limited than the news cycle suggests.
2. **Identify the senior IC who will lead the eval.** They should be available, technically credible to your team, and not currently overloaded. Brief them before the all-hands.
3. **Talk to 2-3 trusted senior engineers** before the all-hands. Get their honest read. If they think you should pivot, you might be wrong about the situation.
4. **Decide the date for the eval result.** Not "in a few weeks." A specific date.
5. **Pre-write the customer talking points** with sales / CS leadership. Customers will ask within 72 hours; you don't want to be improvising.
